        Regional Games is the Asian, Commonwealth, European, Pan American and African Games — the individual events only. BWF has filed the Asian Games under four different categories and under none at all, so they are recognised by name; the team editions are separate tournaments and are left out with every other team event. Sub-regional games — East Asian, Mediterranean, SEA — are not in it.

        Unmapped is everything from before 2007, and it has no rung because there was no ladder. Until the Superseries began that season the circuit was the IBF World Grand Prix, which graded its events 6-star down to 1-star by prize money alone — and the money was not the prestige: the 2006 All England was a 4-star on $125,000, two rungs under the China Masters at $250,000. Nothing turns a purse into a tier, so these titles are shown together, last, in one section that says plainly that its placing is a guess. BWF's own filing is no help: of 85 tournaments from before 2007, checked across six careers of that era, 77 carry the same category id — one bucket holding a world championship, an All England and a $3,000 satellite alike.
        Results stop at 2006 because that is where BWF's own records stop carrying a position. Earlier seasons come back as a list of tournaments with no result beside them, and a cell that can only say “Played” is not a career.

        Each block is one level, and the cells in it are that season's results at that level, best first — not one column per tournament. A block is as wide as the most anyone here played at that level in a single season, so the empty cells on the right of a block are events they did not enter that year. Hover any cell for the tournament and the result.

        * Read in Superseries names, the modern results are the translated ones: Super 1000 as Superseries Premier, Super 750 as Superseries, Super 300 as Grand Prix Gold and Super 100 as Grand Prix, traced through the tournaments themselves. Those squares carry the notch now, and everything from before 2018 is simply called what it was. ⚠ The Super 500 has no rung here. Followed backwards across 2018 it splits four to three between Grand Prix Gold and Superseries, because one Grand Prix Gold became both a Super 500 and a Super 300 — 2018 was a split, not a renaming. A Super 500 is folded up into the Superseries and is therefore drawn one rung higher than it was. That is the one thing this reading gets wrong, and it is why those squares are notched too. The gap between Superseries and Grand Prix Gold is the Super 500 rung left standing empty rather than closed up.

        What each season held

        A score of 100 is the whole season — every title on the board that year, and nobody else with one. It is a share, so one player's rise is always somebody else's fall, and the seasons can be held against each other however many titles each of them held.
        Not every title counts the same. Each is worth what its rung on the board is worth, and the rungs step by φ, the golden ratio — 1.618, the same ladder the photographs above are sized by. So the score is not a second ranking; it is the board's own ranking, added up. A Super 750 is the unit: Seven Super 750s to an Olympic gold, which is about the trade anybody who has watched the sport would make. Seasons before 2018 are weighed on the same five rungs and named for what they were called at the time — 2013 held twelve Superseries where 2023 holds six Super 750s — so a title is never called something it was not, and never weighed on a ladder of its own.
        ⚠️ A competitor has a point only in the seasons they won something. This data says who won, not who entered, so “nothing in 2013” and “not on the tour in 2013” cannot be told apart — and a line drawn along the bottom would be claiming the first while knowing only the second. Lines break at the gaps, and a lone season stays a lone face.
        ⚠️ In the doubles draws a competitor is the pair, not the two people in it. A title is won by a partnership; halving it would draw every pair as two identical lines and make the chart describe people rather than teams. So a partner change ends one line and starts another — which is what happened.
        ⚠️ An asterisk marks a season with a hole in it — far fewer titles than the ones around it, so a score built on one is built on less. 2020 stopped in March. 2022 ran eight of these against a normal twelve to fifteen, with not one tournament held in China and the Finals moved out of Guangzhou. The last year is ongoing. The bars under the axis are how many titles each season held, and a dashed leg is a line crossing one of the marked years.
        Show hides the crowd: it draws everybody whose best season clears the bar. Its default is worked out from the data — as high as it will go without dropping anybody who ever led a season — and the season being played is left out of that sum, because a part-played year cannot say who led it. Click a name to pin it — or click the face on the chart, which is the same name with a season attached and does the same thing. The others stay on the chart, dimmed, because a share means nothing without the people it was taken from. Click again to unpin, or press Esc to clear the lot.

        Each square is a title, sized by what it was worth — the same ladder the honours board uses, so a Super 1000 here is a Super 1000 there. The Olympics and the World Championships share the top row, because every season holds one or the other. They are drawn the same size and a gold ring tells them apart — because on a row of faces two sizes read as a layout accident rather than as a ranking. Only the Olympic square is ringed: one marked square beside a plain one is the ranking, and a second ring would have made them a matched pair. Below them the World Tour Finals, then the Super 1000s, then the Super 750s — every other tier says its rank by size alone.
        ⚠️ In the doubles draws a square holds the pair, split down the middle — half a photograph each, and each half is the middle of its own photograph rather than one player's left ear and the other's right. The square stays the size the ladder says it is: a doubles title is won by a partnership, and every square on this board has to mean the same thing. Above the square the pair is one competitor — one tile, one bar, one line on the score — so a player who changes partner starts again, which is the honest reading: the old team stopped winning and a new one started. And a pair is always drawn the same way round: BWF does not list a partnership consistently — Kido and Setiawan appear eleven times one way and once the other — so the page takes the federation's own commonest order for each pair and uses it everywhere. No convention is layered on top of that; BWF itself puts the woman first in some mixed pairs and the man first in others, and this says what the federation says.
        Click a square to follow one competitor across the board — everything else recedes and the picked faces stay lit. Click more to compare them, click a lit one again to drop it, or press Esc to get the whole board back. The bars in the eras band pick the same way, and so do the faces on the score chart. The squares themselves stay: what fades is the photographs, so the shape of every season is still there to read behind them.
        ⚠️ No team events and no regional games. A team title would rank a player by the country they were born in. The Asian Games, the Commonwealth Games and the European Games are in the data and left out for the same reason more quietly: each is closed to most of the world, so counting any one of them picks a region.
        ⚠️ Seasons before 2018 are drawn on the modern ladder — a Superseries Premier where a Super 1000 goes, a Superseries where a Super 750 goes — and each square is named for what it was called at the time, so hovering a 2013 title says Superseries Premier and the same rung in 2023 says Super 1000. Before 2011 there was no Premier tier at all: all twelve Superseries were one rank, so 2007–2010 fill both rows with squares of the same size. The equal size is the point — a larger top row would claim a tier that did not exist for another four years.
        ⚠️ And the board starts at 2007 because that is where the ladder starts. Before the Superseries the circuit was the IBF World Grand Prix, which graded its events 6-star down to 1-star by prize money alone — and the money was not the prestige: the 2006 All England was a 4-star on $125,000 while the China Masters was a 6-star on $250,000. Sizing those squares by what they paid would draw an All England two rungs below a China Masters, so they are not drawn at all rather than drawn wrong. Those seasons are still on the Seasons and Compare pages, grouped as Unmapped.
        ⚠️ A dashed square, and ⁕ beside the year, mark a title that did not happen when its name says. The season-ending Finals belongs to the season it concludes, so the 2010 Superseries Finals and the 2020 World Tour Finals are drawn in 2010 and 2020 though both were played the following January — the same rule the career grid uses, so the two views cannot disagree. The Tokyo 2020 Olympics stays in 2021, where it was won: a Finals is the conclusion of a season already played, and an Olympics is not the conclusion of anything.
        Eras draws a bar across every run of seasons in which somebody won at least three of the titles above — four or five on the picker, one colour per career, and the totals on the hover. Bars overlap on purpose: LEE Chong Wei's decade runs under LIN Dan's and then CHEN Long's, and naming one champion a year would erase exactly that. Runs are consecutive seasons only, so a dip is a gap and 2020 severs every line.

        The whole draw, feeders on the left and the final on the right. The Show from chips fold away the rounds that are over — and they do it by re-laying out the tree from that round, not by hiding columns: every round doubles the gap between its cards, so the four quarter-final cards sit sixteen slots apart to line up with thirty-two first-round matches nobody is looking at any more. Hiding the columns would have left the gaps. It opens on the earliest round that still has a match to play. A dashed card with Bye is not a fixture — that player is through without playing, which at some tournaments is sixteen of the thirty-two first-round cells. Click a card to star the match, the same star the order of play uses. ←→ page · O order of play · B bracket · ↑↓ how much of the draw · MWX discipline, twice for the doubles

        One column per court, and down each one the matches in the order they are played — a row is a moment, so two cards level with each other were on court together. A court that opens a session of its own gets a row to itself and the columns beside it are empty there, because at that moment nothing was on them. Which is not the same as sorting the day by the clock: BWF publishes a start for the first match of each session and then strings a flat 50-minute estimate through the rest, which on some courts runs backwards. Estimated times are marked ≈ and carry BWF's own Followed by; a published one never is. Click a match to star it — starred matches stay lit and the rest stay dimmed, so a handful of them read at a glance across a whole day.
